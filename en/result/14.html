Britannicus, born Tiberius Claudius Caesar Britannicus, was the son of the Roman emperor Claudius and Valeria Messalina, and for a time he stood near the center of the Julio-Claudian succession. Britannicus died in Rome at just 13, reportedly after being poisoned during a dinner shortly before his fourteenth birthday, in a death widely linked by ancient sources to Nero’s determination to remove a rival claimant.

In September 1932, at age 13, he was killed in his native village together with his younger brother, and his death was turned into a political martyr narrative.

Controverial historical figure

Why this is controversial: Simon of Trent was a child and was not personally responsible for any wrongdoing. The controversy is about the posthumous cult and martyr narrative built around his death, which became a major anti-Jewish blood-libel case. Local Jews were falsely accused, tortured, and executed, and the story was used for centuries to promote antisemitic religious propaganda before the Catholic Church suppressed the cult in 1965.

Simon of Trent (Simonino di Trento) was a toddler from 15th-century Trent whose death became one of Europe’s most notorious blood-libel cases. After he disappeared during Easter week in 1475, his body was found a few days later; local Jews were falsely accused, tortured into confessions, and several were executed, while Simon’s cult spread for centuries before being suppressed by the Catholic Church in 1965.

Did you know:

For most of history, a drink of water was also a gamble. Rivers, wells, and town pumps could carry sewage-borne diseases such as cholera and typhoid. In the United States, typhoid fever was about 100 cases per 100,000 people in 1900; after water treatment, sanitation, and disinfection spread, it fell to 33.8 by 1920 and 0.1 by 2006. A glass of boring tap water may be one of the greatest inventions in history.

The humbling part is that this is not ancient history everywhere. WHO estimates that around 1 million people still die each year from diarrhoea linked to unsafe drinking water, sanitation, and hand hygiene. So when someone in a modern city turns a tap, showers, brushes teeth, and flushes a toilet without thinking, they are living inside a public-health miracle.
